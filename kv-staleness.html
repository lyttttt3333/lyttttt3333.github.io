Stale KV, Long RolloutsKV-Cache Staleness in Fully Asynchronous Agentic RL

The prefix cache remembers old weights. What that does to long agentic rollouts, and what fully asynchronous RL costs when the policy itself is stale.

By Yitong LiNovember 2026≈ 20 min read

TL;DR
  • In async RL for multi-turn agents, most of each turn’s context is served from the prefix cache, and common stacks never invalidate that cache when weights change. A shared system-prompt KV can stay at version 0 for the whole run.
  • Stale KV whose age is bounded is harmless in every setting we measured. Stale KV whose age is unbounded causes rare, late, irreversible collapses. On a 0.6B tool-use task, 9 of 15 seeds failed within 3,000 steps against 0 of 25 for clean cache policies. On a 4B multi-turn task, 10 of 13 runs collapsed when KV could age past 64 weight updates, against 0 of 15 when the age was capped at 64 or less.
  • The fix is cheap: give the engine fresh KV after weight updates, either at every update or, at moderate learning rates, at least every ≤ 64 updates. The safe interval shrinks fast as the learning rate grows. Importance-sampling corrections do not help, and the served training reward does not show the failure.
  • Policy staleness behaves the same way: runs that survive pay nothing, but the share of seeds that collapse rises with lag (0% → 20% → 42% → 71% → 100% for lag 0/2/4/6/≥8 at lr 2e-6). Lowering the learning rate or scaling the model removes it, and async then buys 2.3–3× fewer GPU-hours at equal accuracy.

01Why the cache is part of the policy

Synchronous RL alternates: generate a batch, train on it, push the new weights to the inference engines, generate again. Nothing is stale, but generation and training wait for each other. Fully asynchronous RL keeps the engines generating while the trainer updates; every time new weights are pushed (a refit), the policy version goes up by one. The throughput gain is large, and so is the bookkeeping question it raises: which weights produced what?

For multi-turn agents the answer is more tangled than it looks. Each turn re-submits the whole conversation, and the inference engine serves most of it from its prefix cache: in our agent workloads, 95–99% of each turn’s input tokens are cache hits. The mainstream stacks we instrumented (NeMo-RL, open-instruct, AReaL with SGLang) keep that cache across weight updates. With version tags on every token and KV block, we confirmed that a weight update never resets the prefix cache, and that NeMo-RL’s recompute_kv_cache_after_weight_updates is a no-op while requests are running. With the default settings, the KV of the shared system prompt had an age of 297–299 versions at step 300: it was computed by the initial weights and never again. 99% of tokens attended to some stale KV, and 41% of the attention mass fell on that one stale prefix.

Policy lag

Which weights sampled the token?

The trainer at version v receives tokens sampled at v − k. Frameworks cap k (max_trajectory_age in NeMo-RL, eta in AReaL). With in-flight weight updates, one trajectory can contain tokens from several versions, so the lag has to be tracked per token.

KV age

Which weights computed the KV it attended to?

Two sources: the episode’s own earlier turns (bounded by how many versions the episode spans), and prefixes shared across episodes, like the system prompt and tool specs. If the shared prefix is never refreshed, its age grows with training and has no bound.

The distinction matters because the usual off-policy machinery addresses only the first kind. Importance sampling reweights tokens by πtrain/πbehaviour, which corrects for which weights sampled a token. It assumes the behaviour policy is a policy the trainer could evaluate. “Version-n weights reading a version-0 system prompt” is not one of those, and, as we show below, the two can drift apart in ways that matter.

Three ways to treat the cache at a refit

keep
Do nothing. All cached KV is reused across versions. This is the default in the stacks above, and it lets the shared prefix age with training.
flush
Fresh KV after every weight update. No KV computed by old weights is reused once new weights land. This is the clean reference, and the most expensive option (Section 6 covers a cheaper way to implement it).
per-episode salt
Put the episode ID into the cache key. Nothing is shared across episodes, so the system prompt is always fresh, but each episode’s own history is still reused across versions.

Two variants appear later: flush every N keeps the cache but clears it every N refits, so KV age never exceeds N; frozen v0 pins the shared header to the initial weights, as an amplifier.

What policy lag looks like in a real system

Frameworks record one lag number per trajectory: how many versions behind the trainer it was when it started. That is the quantity the age cap (max_trajectory_age) bounds. In all 40 runs below it sat exactly at the cap for 100% of steady-state sequences, so on its own it says nothing. What the trainer actually consumes is the lag of each token, and that depends on the hardware balance as much as on the cap.

Setup. Qwen3-8B in thinking mode on DAPO math, 16 prompts × 8 samples per step, up to 16k output tokens, NeMo-RL with vLLM, with a version tag on every sampled token. We swept five generator/trainer splits (different node counts, plus an injected trainer delay to reach the far right) × age cap 1, 2, 5, 8 × in-flight weight updates on or off. That gives 40 short runs of 12–36 steps, which measure distributions and throughput, not learning.

To build intuition for what sets these distributions, we also wrote a small interactive staleness simulator. It works at the level of whole episodes and keeps only the window η, the episode-length distribution, the number of episodes that can generate at once, and the trainer’s step time.

A large cap does not by itself mean a large lag. At cap 8 on a generation-bound split, the mean token lag was 3.0 versions and no token reached the cap: the engines’ concurrent-sequence slots (128 per engine) fill before the cap binds, and extra in-flight batches just queue. The transition is sharp, and it sits where throughput is best.

So the fastest configuration is also the one where policy lag starts to bind. At cap 8, moving from a ratio of 1.2 to 2.4 cut the step time from 92 to 83 s and raised the mean token lag from 3.0 to 6.4 versions. Tuning the system for throughput pushes it toward the cap. Section 4 measures what each extra version of lag costs.

These are research notes, not a paper. They condense nine study tracks and about 800 training runs, from 0.6B to 235B parameters, on math with a Python tool, a multi-turn lookup task, terminal agents and multi-turn coding. Most arms have 3–15 seeds, horizons are 150–3,000 steps, and we tried only two or three learning rates per setting. Claims marked inference go beyond what we measured directly.

02The failure only shows up on long horizons

Setup. Qwen3-0.6B solves math problems with a multi-turn Python tool. AReaL runs fully async (lag ≤ 2) with SGLang on 2 A100s per run. Every episode shares a ≈ 300-token system + tool header. We compare keep, flush and per-episode salt, plus frozen v0. Under keep, the header is refreshed only as a side effect of job restarts. At lr 1e-6, keep got 15 seeds × 3,000 steps; the clean side got 25 seeds (20 with fresh KV, 5 with per-episode salt), most of them also run to 3,000 steps.

At lr 1e-6, which is close to what production recipes use, keep failed in 9 of 15 seeds, between steps 567 and 2,380. Frozen v₀ failed in 8 of 8. The 25 clean seeds (fresh KV and per-episode salt) had zero failures in 71.8k seed-steps. Keep versus clean: log-rank p = 6×10⁻⁶.

The averaged learning curves hide this. Keep reaches a clean GSM8K score of 0.75 as fast as flush (599 vs 553 steps). By step 1,000 only one keep seed had failed; by step 1,800, seven had. Keep seeds that never failed end exactly where flush does (0.80–0.84). A 500-step study with 3 seeds per arm would have reported “no effect”.

What a failure looks like

Each failure follows the same pattern. The engine-vs-trainer KL (we use the k3 estimator, E[r − 1 − log r]) grows exponentially, e-folding every 10–17 steps and going from below 0.04 to above 1 in about 40 steps. The training reward, computed on what the engine served, stays normal. The clean evaluation, which recomputes the header with the current weights, collapses: the model emits “N N N …”. We found no early warning in entropy, gradient norm or the early gap.

03KV age sets the risk, and bounding it is enough

Keep fails late, and “late” has two readings. Either the model has trained long enough to become fragile, or the shared header has grown old enough. In the keep runs the two clocks come apart by accident. Every job restart empties the engine’s cache, so the header is recomputed with the current weights and its KV age drops to zero, while training carries on from the same step. Restarts were driven by job scheduling, not by anything the model did. Figure 7 lays out every run at the production-like learning rate, together with three arms that flush the cache on a fixed schedule.

Modelling the failure rate on both clocks at once, header age explains the failures (p = 10⁻⁴) and training step adds nothing once age is known (p = 0.30). Each additional 250 versions of age roughly triples the hazard. The mechanism is visible in the train/inference gap: it climbs steadily with header age, and every one of the 37 restart refreshes cut it, to 0.17× of its previous value on median, after which it grew back roughly as age0.6.

Age is the driver, but there is no safe threshold on age alone. Flushing every 512 or 256 versions still produced failures, at header ages of only 108–313 (Figure 7, middle rows). Only the tightest schedule, every 64 versions, had none.

Importance sampling cannot repair this. The effective sample size stayed at 0.96, and only 0.03% of tokens were rejected. The bias is not in individual token weights. It comes from the gap between the served policy (θ + stale KV) and the optimised policy (θ + fresh KV), and per-token ratios cannot see that gap. At 3e-6, no IS source we tried (engine logprobs, logprobs recomputed at the start, or none) made keep safe, and a [0.5, 2] band did not save frozen v₀ either.

The same bound on a larger model

Setup. Qwen3-4B in thinking mode on a multi-turn lookup-and-compute tool task (≈ 7 turns, ≈ 5k tokens per episode), SGLang with a custom trainer, 500 steps, lr 2e-6, 16 prompts × 8 samples, lag ≤ 2. Tool latencies are replayed from real coding-agent traces. Here we rebuild the version-0 header after every restart on purpose, so restarts cannot reset the age.

The picture repeats. Runs whose KV could age past 64 refits mostly collapsed (10 of 13). Runs with the age capped at 16, 32 or 64 refits never did (0 of 15; Fisher p = 2×10⁻⁵). Three caveats come with the bound:

  • It lowers the hazard; it does not guarantee a run. Two uncapped seeds survived to KV age ≈ 140, and one gap-triggered run that never let KV age exceed 20 still collapsed.
  • Collapse is irreversible. A run that flushed every 128 refits collapsed around step 100, before its first flush, and the flush at step 128 did not bring the reward back. The weights were broken, not the cache.
  • The learning rate sets the budget, and the budget shrinks fast. The KV age a run tolerated fell roughly tenfold for each 1e-6 of learning rate (Figure 9). With longer contexts (≈ 8k tokens) at 2e-6, a cap of 16 was needed. On the 0.6B task, raising the learning rate to 2e-6 or 3e-6 made every stale-header seed fail.

Flushing only when the measured gap crosses a threshold was unreliable: at one threshold it fired too late in every run. A fixed cap is simpler and held up on both tasks.

We then repeated the cap sweep at two more learning rates. At 3e-6 a full flush stayed stable in all four seeds, so the failures below are caused by KV age, not by the learning rate alone.

04Policy staleness: the hazard rises with lag

Setup. Qwen3-4B (and 1.7B) on KodCode multi-turn coding: write code, run the visible tests in a sandbox, read the errors, fix, for up to 4 turns, with the full test suite as reward. NeMo-RL, one GB200 node per run, 222 runs. The key design is controlled lag injection: with in-flight updates off, every trained token is exactly k versions stale (k = 0 is synchronous). This gives a clean dose–response curve that does not depend on the hardware split. A collapse is an 8-step mean reward below 0.1.

Our working model is that the hazard depends on learning rate × staleness inference. It fits both families: at lr 1e-6 lag up to 8 is clean, while k = 12 collapsed 3/8 and k = 16 collapsed 5/5. At 4e-6 even synchronous training collapsed in 2 of 3 seeds. The KV-age results point the same way. With two or three learning rates per setting, this is a hypothesis, not a fitted law.

Common mitigations behaved as follows. ICE-POP and sequence-masked TIS did not help. A KL penalty of 0.01 kept runs stable but learned the least. Halving the learning rate worked.

05When runs survive: a quiet cost for stale KV, none for policy lag

Sections 2–4 were about collapses. This section asks what staleness costs in the runs that do not collapse. Here the two kinds of staleness part ways. A stale shared prefix quietly lowers how much the model learns. Policy lag costs nothing measurable, and buys a large cut in compute.

Stale KV: a quieter cost on long episodes

Setup. Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on terminal tasks. To get long episodes we chain 5 tasks in one sandbox per episode (median 17 turns, 6.2k tokens, 130 s, spanning ≈ 2 versions). AReaL fully async (lag ≤ 8), 8 A100s per run, lr 1e-6, 400 steps, scored with fresh KV on 200 held-out tasks.

Here keep did not collapse within 400 steps, but it learned less. The damage came from the shared version-0 prefix, not from the episode’s own 1–2-version-old history: per-episode salt keeps the stale own history and was the best arm. The first 100 steps showed no difference (p = 0.25), which again says the effect needs a long run to appear.

Policy lag: survivors pay nothing, and async is much cheaper

The coding runs that survive pay nothing. At step 400 the surviving k = 2 seeds are +0.6 ± 0.7 percentage points versus sync, and k = 4 is +0.1 ± 1.0. Policy lag does not make learning slower or worse. It makes it riskier, and once that risk is controlled, async is worth it.

  • At lr 1e-6, in-flight age 4 is free. After 800 steps on 4B it matches sync exactly (0.0 ± 1.0 points) and takes 2.3× less time. The same age at lr 2e-6 collapsed 5 of 8 seeds.
  • Scale helps. At 8B the per-step off-policy gap is about 10× smaller than at 4B. Age 8 collapsed 0 of 5 seeds at 32B, while 4 of 5 dipped below their starting accuracy at 4B. At 32B, age 2 is 1.2 points below sync at step 400 (95% CI −4.7 to +2.2): slightly lower, but not significantly so with 5 seeds per arm, at about 3× fewer GPU-hours.
  • In-flight weight updates are worth about 2.4× in step time. Without them, extra lag buys no speed, because every refit drains the pipeline. In sandbox-bound coding RL, a higher age cap buys its throughput out of the sandbox queue: from age 1 to 8, step time falls from 21.1 to 12.0 s while per-episode queueing grows from 2.8 to 21.8 s and decode and execution stay flat.

Flush does not have to clear the cache literally. If the weight version is part of the cache key, entries written under old weights are simply never hit again, while requests within one version still share prefixes. That gives the same fresh KV with 31% less prefill than a hard flush, preempts no running requests, and avoids the interrupt-and-resume path where the engine bug below lived.

Monitoring

  • Do not trust the served reward. It measures θ + stale KV and trails validation by about k steps. We saw it read 0.6–0.8 while validation was already at 0.
  • Run a periodic clean evaluation with fresh KV, and track a rolling train/inference k3. Alarms on the gap were inconsistent across settings. On the 4B tool task the gap gave about 77 steps of warning (10 of 12 collapses caught). On coding, a 20× jump in the 5-step median gave about 15 steps of warning (37 of 62 failing runs caught, 9/158 false alarms). On the 0.6B task we found no usable precursor. Use a fixed cap rather than relying on an alarm.
  • Treat the learning rate and staleness together. A higher learning rate needs a much shorter KV age and a smaller policy lag.

Pitfalls that nearly changed our conclusions

  • Seeds and horizons. Failures arrive late and seed variance is large: one checkpoint’s within-arm standard deviation was 0.04–0.10. Use ≥ 5–7 seeds, window means over several evaluations, and survival analysis rather than mean curves. In async systems the same seed number does not reproduce a run, so seed numbers alone do not make a paired comparison.
  • Restarts silently reset KV. Every job restart flushes the cache, which makes keep look safer than it is in a run that never restarts.
  • Engine bugs can masquerade as findings. In vLLM 0.19.1, a flush with async scheduling enabled drops the first token after a request resumes (vLLM #42043). That made flush look like it had 2.4× the train/inference mismatch of keep. With the fix backported, the excess disappeared. Separately, an SGLang scheduler silently ignored our per-episode cache key at first, so “per-episode salt” was initially a no-op.

07Open questions

  • How the budget scales with the learning rate. On the 4B task the tolerated KV age fell from above 350 at 1e-6 to about 21 at 2e-6 and about 1 at 3e-6, so a product rule like “LR × age” does not fit. On the 0.6B task the boundary sits elsewhere (a cap of ≈ 64 safe at 1e-6, ≈ 8 at 3e-6). Three learning rates on one task, with 2–5 seeds per cell, cannot pin down the form, or say how it moves with model size and context length.
  • Reliable alarms. We do not yet have a gap-based signal that works as a switch in every setting. A fixed cap is the safer default for now.
  • Large models over long horizons. At 30B MoE we ran only 150 steps with one seed per arm. There, keep was 16% faster per step than flush and had a 13–17% larger gap, with no runaway. At 235B we have not trained with a stale cache. Scale helped policy staleness a lot. Whether it helps KV staleness equally is open.
